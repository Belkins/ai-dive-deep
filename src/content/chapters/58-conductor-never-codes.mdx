At 16:47 UTC on 6 October I typed this into a Claude Code session: "No lets start to build it NOW, launch 20 agents simultaniesy with workflows to make this live asap". Three days later, at 17:35 on 9 October, five sessions were live on my Mac at the same moment. Two were building Rightmark. Three were building Dylan. Across 18 days and 20 sessions I typed 223 prompts, and the median one was 52 characters long.

The agents were never the hard part. The system around them was, and every rule in it was paid for by a failure you can read below.

## Two products, one Mac

Rightmark helps companies license their private data to AI developers, and it pays the people who introduce them, the Rightmark Scouts. Its home page states the thesis: "AI labs have read the public internet. Now they pay for what is private." An owner gets an appraisal in about a minute, with no files and no access. Rightmark's build ran as a seven-day burst from 6 October, in two long sessions.

Dylan is an AI SDR. His page title says it: "the AI SDR that books B2B meetings, billed when held." He finds buyers, writes to them from his own mailboxes, answers their replies and books qualified meetings into your calendar. The site went live on 8 October at 12:20, under the eyebrow line "AI agent · Built by Belkins". His build ran in eighteen sessions, across the site and the platform.

The builds ran beside each other. Two or more sessions were live for about 70.8 hours, three or more for about 26.8 and four or more for about 8.2. Seventeen of my prompts contained "continue", and 15 asked some form of "what is left". Run two builds at once only if each one can go a day without you.

## The conductor never codes

The Rightmark build doc opens on one rule: "The main Claude Code session is the conductor. It plans, delegates, merges through one integrator, and reports. It does not write application code."

It casts the <GlossaryTerm term="Swarm">swarm</GlossaryTerm> as a film shoot: "The conductor is the director. The architect is the production designer who draws the sets before anyone builds." Ten roles make up the crew: conductor, architect, builder, designer, writer, verifier, security, leak-guard, red-team and integrator. Builders, designers, writers and verifiers each get a <GlossaryTerm term="Worktree">worktree</GlossaryTerm>.

Dylan's build kit says it in four sentences: "You lead. Specialists build. Independent agents check. Scripts hold the plan." It names eight roles, each with a model and an effort level. The architect, the security reviewer and the skeptic run on Opus at xhigh. The web, app and engine builders and the content editor run on Opus at high. The QA verifier runs on Sonnet at high.

Almost none of it was hand-spawned. My main sessions made 132 Workflow calls and only 27 plain Agent calls: the fan-out of [Chapter 6](/chapters/06-the-swarm/), written down as [dynamic workflows](/dynamic-workflows/), with the patterns from [/swarms/](/swarms/) underneath.

Rightmark ran as two sessions that talked: 116 cross-session messages arrived between them, and the second queued approved units for the builder. It also watched production. At 10:30 on 9 October it reported that the sign-in pages had been answering 500 since a morning deploy. A second session watching production is a checker nobody has to schedule.

Give the lead one job: decide. The conductor prompt and role roster are templates in [the Swarm Build Playbook](/swarm-build-playbook/).

## A plan someone else can check

Rightmark's Phase 0 planned with competing drafts. Three architects drafted independently, one optimizing for delivery speed, one for security and privacy, one for SEO and performance. A judge merged them, then three critics and a refuter attacked the merge. Out came 51 architecture decision records, 41 TypeScript contract files and a dependency graph of 90 units.

Every unit in that graph is a card with the same fields: id, deps, owns, reads, acceptance, evidence and review. A builder knows what it may touch, a verifier knows what done looks like. Shared files belong only to integrator units.

Dylan's planning added one habit: pilot every workflow on one unit first. The Phase 0 pilot lane cost 297,733 tokens against 260,000 estimated and checked 58 facts: 47 confirmed, 11 corrected, 0 refuted.

Decisions ran on defaults. Rightmark's decisions file says it in one line: "The swarm uses the default until Vlad decides." The conductor batched open questions to the next gate instead of stopping the graph, and by 12 October the file held 27 decision rows and 24 dated answers.

If a verifier cannot read done off the card, the unit is not planned yet.

## Four checkers for every builder

Rightmark's build doc has four quality patterns. The first matters most: "The agent that built a unit never grades it. A fresh verifier checks out the branch, reruns every command, and tries the feature like a user." The second: every finding goes to a second agent whose job is to disprove it, and only survivors become fix units.

From 6 to 12 October, 85 Rightmark workflow runs logged 3,021 agent starts. By label: 434 verify, 334 refute, 320 leak-guard and 265 security, against 337 build. That is 1,353 checker starts, four for every builder.

The checkers had a failure of their own first. On 6 October the scaffold unit, the one every other unit depended on, burned three hours: three verify rounds and two fix rounds. It came back blocked with zero P0 or P1 findings. The verifier kept inventing new bypasses, and the whole graph waited. The conductor read a blocked unit with nothing serious in it and saw the goalposts moving.

The fix was a severity rubric. Verifiers now grade each unmet criterion by severity, and only P0 and P1 gaps block. The scaffold was accepted later that day, with a parallel hardening unit.

Dylan's kit caps the same loop from the other side. A finding is a blocker or a major only if it cites a gate, a promise or a failing test. After three fix rounds the unit is "stuck": "Never loop a fourth time." Reviewers never fix what they found; a fresh builder does.

The <GlossaryTerm term="Review fleet">review fleet</GlossaryTerm> earned that rule in Phase 4. Round 2 found focus lost on a failed submit, and a guard test that could not fail. The kit already said what a guard test owes you: the reviewer removes the guard locally and watches the test go red.

<PullQuote>Tests that cannot fail prove nothing.</PullQuote>

## One lock on main

Rightmark's parallel runs shared one main branch through one merge lock. The integrator takes the lock, merges with no commit, runs the quick gate on the staged merge, commits, marks the unit merged and removes its worktree. Anything that fails aborts the merge and leaves main untouched: the integrator <GlossaryTerm term="Fail closed">fails closed</GlossaryTerm>.

The run generator says why in a parenthesis.

<PullQuote>A merge commit diffs nothing.</PullQuote>

Gate the staged merge and you test what main is about to become.

9 October tested the lock four ways. First, dead syncs: a run learns of other runs' merges only through sync agents, and after about 4 hours of quiet it gave up. Five runs died that way and stranded 54 units that never started. The generator now allows 64 idle syncs by default, about 17 hours.

Second, a sync agent wrote a stray file into the main checkout and, in the generator's words, "blocked every merge". Sync agents may no longer write there.

Third, contention. At a load average near 400 a gate on a staged merge ran 50 minutes, and a waiting integrator decided the lock was stale and took it mid-merge. A keeper script now refreshes the lock during long merges.

Fourth, merges failed on vitest's 5-second default at load averages of 40 to 180. Known-slow tests got explicit 30-second limits.

On 10 October I pushed: "please i need all features on my website be ready now". Two runs restarted with more slots. The same day, resuming a stopped parallel run replayed reviews that had already finished, because the resume cache is positional. The conductor's README now carries the rule: "Never resume a stopped run: the resume cache is positional, so start a fresh one".

## Production is the last test

Between 8 and 12 October Rightmark's deploy script shipped 201 production deploys, every one smoke-tested: 20, 71, 47, 49 and 14 a day. A watcher woke every 10 minutes. When main had moved, it exported main cleanly, never the working checkout or a peer's edits, deployed it, smoke-tested key pages and every sitemap URL, and rolled back on a failure. After three failed deploys in a row it stops.

Two further attempts failed, and both were caught. On 8 October one deploy failed and production stayed unchanged. On 9 October a smoke test found /sign-in/ answering 500, and the watcher rolled back on its own.

The same day a nested-layout redirect sent Scouts to the wrong page after sign-in, and a live GET check found it, not the tests.

The host's Git trigger never deployed Dylan's Phase 3 merge: no deployment, no commit status. The lead deployed that exact commit from Git through the host's API. Then a production end-to-end found every waitlist step after the first answering 503: the storage served a weak etag that the update check never matched. Local tests had used a file store, which is why they never saw it.

[Chapter 53](/chapters/53-first-real-caller/) found the same pattern in a different build, where production caught what 601 passing tests missed. Smoke every URL on every deploy, roll back without asking, and run one end-to-end on production against the real store.

## Taste is not a gate

On 8 October rightmark.ai went live, and the same day I sent the homepage back.

<PullQuote>“its not the website we initially design, that is not the main page i prepared at the beggining”</PullQuote>

On 4 October, before any platform code, five parallel builders had made a 56-page static prototype, and an independent review had checked it. I approved that design. Then the conductor applied its own brief rules over my copy, and a style rule quietly rewrote my approved hero. Each rule made sense on its own.

The fix was to make the 4 October design binding: the reference for every page it contains, where only hard rules may force a difference, each one listed. The hero keeps "See what yours is worth". Fifteen parity units brought the rest back, all 15 merged, and parity joined the full gate, so the next drift shows up red.

Dylan's design started with options. Five designer agents worked in parallel from one brief, one direction each, and with the existing first site that made six. The winner, The Meeting, starts from the only thing a client pays for: the page is a week calendar, and time is the spacing unit. In the search-pages run a fixer reverted page titles, and the lead restored them.

[Chapter 46](/chapters/46-designing-with-ai/) argues a model cannot pick the right interface; [Chapter 52](/chapters/52-what-agents-cant-see/) found a design nobody rendered. Here a chosen design was rewritten one sensible rule at a time. Write taste down as a reference, and put parity in the gate.

## I automated the clicks and kept the signatures

Rightmark's build doc lists what always needs my explicit OK in chat: production deploys, domains and DNS, purchases, emails or messages to real people, pushes to a remote, publishing anything, accepting terms, and changes to the guardrail files. A content firewall scans source and build output for names and phrases that must never ship. On day one a probe found it missed two banned phrases, and the fix went into a content-lint unit. The legal pages and Scout terms are drafts for counsel review, and Rightmark takes no health data until a HIPAA program and vendor agreements are in place.

Dylan's kit draws its line in two <GlossaryTerm term="Hook">hooks</GlossaryTerm>. The first blocks pushes to main, force pushes, check-skipping merges, production deploys, DNS writes, live payment mode, secret reads, dashes in commits and edits to the guardrails. Its header calls it "a seatbelt, not a security boundary." The second blocks new dashes and secrets in files. On install, five probe payloads were refused. Live payments stay off until counsel approves the Terms, and the page promises that the client approves the plan, the first emails of every new angle and any new spend.

Clicks were different. "Please i dont wnat to do anything manualy use chrome and do all of it", I wrote on 9 October, and the session in charge installed the sheet's script itself. A day later I drew the line from the other side: "i will handle leads, payments manualy for now". I refused manual clicks and kept the money.

[Chapter 38](/chapters/38-run-until-done/) is about letting an agent run until a goal is met. This is its other half: the short list it never touches alone. Automate the clicks, keep the signatures.

## The machine is the budget

11 October was the day the Mac said no. Rightmark's parallel slots across runs summed to 11, the load average hit 460, and gates timed out. A test leak filled the disk; 12.6 GB was cleared, and the watcher now alerts below 12 GiB. A scheduler bug froze one run for four hours, so a watcher now flags any run idle for 75 minutes. A stale 906 MB build folder in the main checkout held the lock for more than 80 minutes. The budget that came out of it: about 5 units in flight, a disk floor and an idle watcher.

Dylan's platform waves hit the same wall that day: a load average of about 900, a dead Docker daemon and 32 orphaned test workers. CI became the gate, and agents stopped running the full local suite. Turn caps were a limit too: the architect at 80 turns and the skeptic at 50 ran out and returned nothing, so every prompt now states a tool budget.

The clock rationed the work as well: one Dylan prompt on 9 October paced it against the 5-hour usage window. On 7 October, and on the morning of 12 October up to the snapshot, the swarms kept building with zero prompts from me.

On 10 October Dylan's site turned out to be invisible: "URL is unknown to Google". An audit named the gap as search demand: the pages buyers search for had never been built. A 12-agent run in four lanes built 26 of them and took the sitemap from 54 pages to 80, at 2.42M tokens against about 1.3M estimated, 1.9 times.

Most of the last column became a template in [the playbook](/swarm-build-playbook/).

<Callout type="warn" title="The rule">
**Make the session in charge a conductor that never codes, and turn every failure into a script, a gate or a role before the next unit starts.**
</Callout>

## What it cost, and what I can't show you

Rightmark's Phase 0 estimate was 90 units, about 512 to 520 agents and about 69M to 140M tokens. By 12 October the plan held 304 units, 212 merged, and 105 of the 304 were fix units. The 75 finished workflows ran 2,284 agents and used about 316.0M tokens, a floor that leaves out runs still live and both main sessions. Even as a floor that is 3.4 times the units, 4.4 times the agents and 2.3 times the top of the token range. The repo took 2,106 commits in seven days.

Dylan's Phase 0 full run took 126 agents and 21.8M tokens against an estimate of 12M, 1.82 times, over the kit's own 1.5 times line. The lead reported the cause: 14 decision records instead of 10, more agents, more reading. Phase 4 was estimated at 10M to 14M tokens and Phase 2 at 6M to 8M; neither actual was recorded. [Chapter 29](/chapters/29-cost-economics/) does the token maths.

What is missing is missing on purpose. I did not meter the main sessions' tokens, so every total undercounts. There is no cost in money. Unit ids, run names, accounts and people are left out.

Rightmark today: the public site, the appraisal, the Scout program's first milestone, Repo Check v2.1.0, the Price Index and the intake form are live. Owner, buyer, partner and console features, hardening, the red team and the launch runbook are not built yet, and the roadmap gives no dates on purpose.

Dylan today: a waitlist, beta pricing, payments only in test mode, and no results of his own yet: no meetings, reply rates or customers to report.

## The closer

A verifier with no stop rule, a style rule with no reference, a lock with no keeper, a file store that was not production. Each gap is now closed by something the system does on its own.

[Chapter 25](/chapters/25-evals-or-hope/) is about an output that stayed broken because nothing was watching it. In a swarm build the watching is the system, and one person still decides what the product looks like and what it may sign.

The agents build the product. I build the thing that builds it.
